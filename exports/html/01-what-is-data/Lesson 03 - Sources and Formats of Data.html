Common sources of data

A data source is where data originally comes from. Data can be collected in many ways:

Manual entry
someone types data into a form or spreadsheet
Business systems
sales, inventory, or HR software
Websites and apps
clicks, sign-ups, purchases
Sensors and devices
weather stations, fitness trackers, machines
Surveys and interviews
customer or employee feedback
Public datasets
government or research data shared openly

The source affects data quality. Data from a reliable business system may be more consistent than data typed manually into a spreadsheet.

Primary vs secondary data

Primary data

Primary data is collected firsthand for a specific purpose: a shop owner recording daily sales in a notebook, or a school conducting its own student survey.

collected firsthand

Secondary data

Secondary data was collected by someone else and is reused for a new purpose: government census reports, industry research, or sales data exported from another department.

collected elsewhere, reused here

Primary data is often more relevant to your exact question. Secondary data can save time, but you must check whether it fits your needs and whether you trust the original source.

A data format is how data is stored or shared.

As a beginner, you will most often encounter spreadsheets, CSV files, and database tables.

Why source and format matter

Ask two questions about any dataset before you use it. Where did this come from? tells you whether to trust it. What format is it in? tells you what it will cost you to open.

The two answers are independent, which is what makes both questions worth asking:

Sales data from a company database usually answers both well - trusted source, easy to open
A PDF report can be entirely trustworthy and still cost you a day to get the numbers out of
Survey responses can arrive in a clean spreadsheet and still be full of missing answers
